THE ARGUMENT OF ALPÖGE’S FORMAL PROOF OF THE RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS: AN ACCOUNT READ FROM THE LEAN SOURCE QUI VINCIT AND FABLE Abstract. On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge released a Lean 4 formalisation, 17 million lines long, of a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis and of its analogue for every Dirichlet L-function, with a note saying that no human-readable proof has been written. We built the archive, twice. It compiles, the five top theorems depend on no axiom beyond the three on which Mathlib rests, and the kernel accepts every theorem, and every definition that is not marked unsafe or partial, of the import closure, 2.7 million declarations in all, replayed from an empty environment by Lean’s own checker and by a program of ours. This paper says what the argument is, as read from the Lean source. The exponent 12 comes from one optimisation: an upper bound, of the shape x/m + m, for a weighted count of prime powers whose Frobenius lies outside a CM type, where m is the length of a chain of theta lifts between definite unitary groups. The bound is a squeeze between two slopes of Hermitian lattices. Divisibility of periods at inert primes, which comes from the height-two formal group of a CM elliptic curve, raises the slopes of lattices of algebraic modular forms. A slope inequality for nearly tight frames of maps, which we state in full, transfers that to a lattice of periods. The slope of that lattice is bounded above by a period computation that returns exactly the main term of the count, through a dimension count and the Chowla–Selberg formula. We describe each step and each input, determine by machine which parts of the archive the final theorem uses, and identify the published theorems on which the proof rests. Contents 1. Introduction 2. Introduction On 20 November 2026 Levent Alpöge released a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis [Rie59], and of the generalised Riemann Hypothesis for the L-functions of Dirichlet [Lej37], formalised in Lean 4 [MU21] on top of Mathlib [The20]. The release consists of the Lean source [Alp26a], 175,187 Lean files and 17,374,215 lines, and a four-page note [Alp26b] with a timeline of twenty steps. The note says that no human-readable proof has been written. This paper does two things. It reports that the proof is correct as a formal object: we built the archive, it compiles, its five top theorems depend on the three standard axioms and nothing else, and the kernel accepts every theorem, and every definition that is not marked unsafe or partial, of the import closure, replayed from an empty environment by Lean’s own checker and by a program of ours (Section 2). And it says what the argument is: an account read from the Lean source, from the final theorem down to the theorems of the published literature on which it rests. The release does not contain one. 1.1. The argument. Fix a primitive Dirichlet character χ. The proof has four movements. Reduction to a one-sided bound on windows. A windowed form of Landau’s theorem reduces the non-vanishing of L(s, χ) for Re s > 12 to a bound G(x) ≤ x 1/2+ε for a smoothed sum over prime powers, required only for x in long windows (Section 4.1). An exact identity. For an auxiliary abelian CM field with CM type Φ, a weighted count ASK of the prime powers whose Frobenius lies outside Φ equals a main term MAIN plus a combination of such sums (Section 4.2). After averaging, an upper bound ASK ≤ MAIN + Err becomes the bound on G, and the error √ has three parts of sizes x/m, m and a rank term, where m is a parameter. At m ≈ x they balance. This is the only place where 12 is produced (Section 4.4). A squeeze between two slopes. The upper bound is proved with Hermitian lattices. For each of many indices u there is a lattice M u of algebraic modular forms on a definite unitary group, and a map B u sending a form to the periods of its m-fold iterated theta lift; m is the number of lifts. At a prime p inert in the CM field those periods are divisible by about 3 p a/p+a/p +··· , where a is the order of the Taylor coefficients of theta functions through which they are expressed, because a CM elliptic curve has a formal group of height two there (Section 6). So B u still maps into the lattice P of periods a lattice N u , obtained by rescaling a sublattice of M u , whose slope exceeds that of M u by at least the count ASK, up to errors and to correction terms that cancel later. The maps B u form a nearly tight frame, by a relative trace formula for Bessel–Poincaré series (Section 7), and a slope inequality for such frames bounds the slope of P from below by the mean slope of the N u (Section 5.4). From above, the slope of P is bounded by m times log(a!/Ω a ), with Ω a CM period, less a term from an auxiliary level prime; a dimension count for that prime and the Chowla–Selberg formula for the mean of log Ω turn this into MAIN and a remainder that the correction terms cancel (Section 5.5). Three inputs. The construction needs a bound on Galois cohomology in a cyclotomic tower, which the note calls a weakening of Vandiver’s conjecture (Section 8); the existence of suitable auxiliary fields at every scale in the windows (Section 9); and a body of theorems on unitary groups, Shimura varieties and Arakelov geometry, each of which the archive proves for itself (Section 10). The shape x/m + m is that of Stepanov’s method [Ste69; Bom74; Sch76], where a polynomial is made to vanish to order m at the points to be counted. Here the multiplicity is the length of a chain of theta lifts, and the degree bound is replaced by slopes of lattices in the sense of Bost [Bos96; Bos01; Cha02]. 1.2. Findings. Beyond the outline, the reading establishes the following.
THE ARGUMENT OF A FORMAL PROOF OF THE RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS 3 (i) The statement proved is Mathlib’s RiemannHypothesis, and the statements for Dirichlet L-functions cover every character of every modulus (Section 2.3). (ii) The final theorem uses 1,075,015 of the archive’s 1,548,656 theorems. Outside its cone are earlier forms of several steps, some with their proofs, and conditional statements, among them one that would give only Re s > 34 (Section 11). (iii) The central inequality is a theorem about Hermitian lattices alone, a slope inequality for nearly tight frames of maps, which we state in full (Statement 5.1). (iv) The period side returns the analytic main term identically; there is no discrepancy term (Statement 5.2). (v) The slope argument depends on arithmetic intersection theory on a Shimura variety, through the covolume of lattices of integral forms; this is why resolution of singularities and an arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel theorem are among the results the archive formalises (Section 5.5). (vi) The cohomological bound is needed only in the weak form “at most exp(x ε )”. The archive proves more, by a route through congruences between cusp forms and Eisenstein classes on U(3); no statement of the main conjecture of Iwasawa theory appears among the results it takes from the literature (Section 8). (vii) The Galois representations the proof needs are constructed in the archive from theta lifts between definite unitary groups, with less local–global compatibility than the literature provides, and the argument is arranged to need no more (Section 8.4). (viii) The proof contains its own formalisations of 179 named results of the literature, among them global class field theory, the Siegel–Weil formula and Hironaka’s theorem (Section 10); beside these the archive proves the theorem of Ferrero and Washington and Kato’s Euler system (Section 8). 1.3. Scope. This is an exposition of Alpöge’s proof, written from its source; the mathematics is his and that of the model he worked with. We do not reprove the theorem, and the certificate of its truth is the kernel’s, not ours. Section 12 states how the reading was done, and Appendix A records where each statement of this paper was read. 2. Main.lean is 12 lines long and proves five theorems. Statement 2.1 (riemannHypothesis). For every s ∈ C with ζ(s) = 0, if s is not of the form −2(n + 1) with n ∈ N and s ̸ = 1, then Re s = 2 1 . The statement is the proposition RiemannHypothesis of Mathlib, not a definition of the archive: in the compiled environment that name is declared in the module Mathlib. NumberTheory.LSeries.RiemannZeta, as is riemannZeta. Statement 2.2 (LFunction_ne_zero_of_half_lt_re). For every modulus N ≥ 1, every Dirichlet character χ modulo N and every s ∈ C with Re s > 12 , if χ ̸ = 1 or s ̸ = 1 then L(s, χ) ̸ = 0. Statement 2.3 (LFunction_eq_zero_re). For every modulus N ≥ 1, every Dirichlet character χ modulo N and every s with 0 < Re s < 1 and L(s, χ) = 0, one has Re s = 12 . The remaining two are riemannZeta_eq_zero_re, which is Statement 2.1 again, and LFunction_eq_zero_re_of_isPrimitive, which is Statement 2.3 for primitive characters. Here L(s, χ) is Mathlib’s DirichletCharacter.LFunction, the meromorphic continuation. The characters are not assumed primitive; restricting to the open strip in Statement 2.3 excludes the zeros on Re s = 0 that imprimitive characters have, so the statements are correctly posed. “GRH” in this paper always means Statements 2.2 and 2.3: Dirichlet L-functions. The note says the case of Dedekind zeta functions is expected and not done. 2.4. The build. We built the unmodified archive twice, on two rented machines. For each of the five theorems, #print axioms reports exactly propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound, the three axioms on which Mathlib as a whole rests. In particular none depends on sorryAx or on an axiom that trusts the compiler. For a theorem of an imported module this command does not walk the terms. It prints a list that Lean computed and recorded when the module that declares the theorem was compiled, from the terms of that module and from the lists recorded for the modules it imports; it walks the terms only where no list is recorded (Lean/Util/CollectAxioms.lean in the source of Lean 4.33.1). So the report is as good as the compiled files it reads, and in the first build those of Mathlib came from the community’s cache. Two programs of ours compute the axioms again from the terms. The replay of Section 2.7 does it for each of the five theorems, in the environment that the kernel built, and finds the same three. The program that computes the cone of Section 2.6 finds these three and no other axiom among the constants of the cone of the five theorems. 2.6. The dependency cone. A build proves that every file compiles; it does not say which declarations the final theorem uses. From the compiled environment we computed the set of all constants reachable from riemannHypothesis through the type and the value of every declaration reached, the link between an inductive type and its constructors, and the structure that a projection belongs to. It has 1,478,220 elements. Of the 1,548,656 theorems the archive declares, 1,075,015 are in it, and the cone meets 147,489 of the 175,014 modules that declare anything. These are counts of names: Lean lets an auxiliary theorem be declared again in each module that needs it, and 39,597 of the archive’s theorem declarations repeat a name declared in another module. When this paper says that a declaration is, or is not, used by the proof, it means membership in this set. Section 11 lists what is outside it. 2.7. Kernel replay. Some words of Lean are needed here. The kernel is the small program that checks a declaration. An inductive type comes with its constructors and with a recursor, its induction principle; the kernel derives these from the declaration of the type, and it alone provides the four constants of the quotient type. An opaque constant is a definition whose value later declarations cannot use. Lean marks a declaration unsafe or partial when
6 QUI VINCIT AND FABLE it is a program and not a statement; no theorem may use one. A file that begins with the word module uses Lean’s module system, by which a file shows the files that import it a reduced version of its declarations. Mathlib and Lean’s library use it; the archive does not (Section 2.2). A build normally checks each declaration once, in the process that elaborated it; code that a file runs while it is compiled can add a declaration that the kernel never sees (Section 2.8). A replay passes the declarations to the kernel a second time, from the compiled files, in a process that does nothing else. We replayed the whole import closure of Main.lean into an environment that starts empty: the archive, Mathlib with its dependencies, and Lean’s own library, 186,200 modules that declare 2,701,138 distinct names. Of these, 2,695,298 are neither unsafe nor partial, and they are what a replay examines, together with the further declarations of names that more than one module declares. Each kind is examined in its own way. A theorem, a definition, an opaque constant or an axiom is passed to the kernel’s entry point Kernel.Environment.addDeclCore against an environment that contains only declarations that precede it in a dependency order. An inductive type is passed with the other types of its mutual block and their constructors, once for the block, and the kernel generates the constructors and the recursors itself. The quotient type is one declaration of the kernel’s, which provides four constants. When every declaration has been sent, the environment the kernel has built is compared with what was read from the compiled files, in both directions: each of the 2,695,298 constants must equal, field by field, the constant of the same name in the kernel’s environment, and the kernel’s environment must contain nothing else. Terms are compared up to the names and annotations of bound variables, which the kernel does not consult. So, apart from the unsafe and partial declarations, the compiled files hold, for the version of each name that Lean’s import keeps and as Lean’s reader of compiled files presents them to a file outside Lean’s module system, exactly what the kernel produces from the declarations in them; and every further version of a repeated name was accepted by the kernel. What a module inside the module system would receive from the same files is compared separately (Section 2.8); in this run that covered 657,071 constants in 11,013 modules. The kernel accepted every declaration sent to it: 2,084,262 theorems, 556,318 definitions, 3,882 opaque constants, 7 axioms, the quotient type with its 4 constants, and 15,769 inductive types in 15,734 blocks. The comparison found every one of the 2,695,298 constants of the files, among them 19,159 constructors and 15,897 recursors, equal to the kernel’s, and nothing else in the kernel’s environment. There was no failure. The run took 116 minutes on 32 threads. The 7 axioms are all declared in Lean’s core library; the archive declares none, and the five theorems depend on three of them. In 40,595 cases a name is declared again by a further module, which Lean’s import accepts for theorems with the same statement; every version was checked. The 5,840 declarations not replayed are those that Lean marks unsafe or partial: 5,008 are the compiled forms of recursive definitions and the rest are programs and the unsafe axioms that Lean’s compiler uses. Lean’s own replay does not check them either, and outside Lean’s module system the kernel accepts no theorem or safe definition that refers to one; Section 2.8 says what holds inside it. For each of the five theorems the program also reports that it was checked as a theorem and that nothing it depends on failed, and computes the axioms it depends on from the terms, in the environment the kernel built. They are propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound for each, and the program that computes the cone finds the same three among the constants of the cone. This matters because #print axioms, for a theorem of an imported module, prints a list that was recorded in the compiled file when it was written; it does not walk the terms again. The checker. The replay was done by a program of ours, of 594 lines, published with this paper. It imports once, orders the declarations, and runs the kernel checks of theorems, definitions, opaque constants and axioms in parallel, each against a snapshot of the environment as it stood when the declaration’s turn came; inductive types and the quotient type are checked on the main thread. Lean’s own checker is leanchecker [MU23]. Its ordinary mode imports the import closure again for every module it checks. Run as described below, 8 processes at a time and beside the other runs named there, it took 61 minutes for 501 names; at that rate, checking each of the 175,187 modules once would take about 15 days on that machine. Its function for one module, called on one module at a time, took 2 to 3 seconds on each of the 30 modules named below. Its other mode, --fresh, replays one closure in one process, as our program does, but on one thread. We ran it on the whole import closure of Main, on the second machine. It accepted the closure, ending with exit code 0 after 8.0 hours, 7.3 of them processor time, and printing nothing; for about its first two hours three of the runs below ran beside it: the sample run whose time is quoted, the second implementation on the closure of the L-function, and the checks of single modules. So each of the 2,695,298 constants of Section 2.7, which are those that are neither unsafe nor partial, each in the version that Lean’s import keeps of its name, has been accepted by the kernel twice from an empty environment, once driven by Lean’s own checker and once by our program. The program has been reviewed four times against the source of Lean 4.33.1, by further instances of the language model that wrote it; no person has reviewed it. All four reports are published with this paper. The first two, made independently of each other, found among other things that the program used for the first two versions of this paper compared only constructors and recursors with the kernel’s, so that an inductive type or a quotient constant written directly into a compiled file was counted and never examined; and that it rebuilt an inductive type from those of its listed constructors that it found, dropping one that was missing. Neither found a way for this to affect the five theorems, and Lean’s own replay shares the first gap. But a replay with those gaps establishes less than those versions said. The program was rewritten along the lines the two reviews proposed. The third review, which had the first two before it, built and ran the rewritten program. It found one further gap. Mathlib and Lean’s library use Lean’s module system, in which a module is compiled to several files, and the program read only the one that a file outside the module system reads; a false theorem in another would not have been seen. It also found that two of the program’s checks were exercised by no test module. The program was changed to compare the other files, and test modules were added. The fourth review, of those changes, found two more routes of the same kind, by which a module inside the module system could receive a constant that the program had not seen, and found that the test script could report on files left by an earlier run. Statement 2.1 follows from Statement 2.2 for the trivial character by the functional equation, in a few dozen lines on Mathlib’s interface. Green [Gre55], used to count local types; • VWin: a bound on Galois cohomology groups attached to an auxiliary field, valid on windows of scales (Section 8); • HypsAlt: a record of forty-six fields about families of lattices of automorphic forms on unitary groups (Sections 5, 7 and 10). It was written by reading that source from the last line upward: each hypothesis was followed to the declaration that supplies it, and each definition was transcribed into ordinary notation, until every branch reached a theorem of the published literature. Three rules governed the reading. Definitions are transcribed, not inferred from names. Names in the source are labels. The same name is used for different propositions in different namespaces, and several formulations of one step coexist. Membership in the proof is decided by the compiled environment. Almost every file of the archive imports almost every other, so the text alone cannot show that a declaration is used by the final theorem. The dependency cone of Section 2.6 decides it by machine, and every statement in this paper that something is or is not used by the proof refers to it. Every statement about the proof has a recorded basis. Appendix A lists the statements of this paper with the file and line where each was read. The paper does not reprove anything, and it describes what the proof does, not how it was found. Constants and lower-order terms are abbreviated; each inequality displayed here holds in the source with explicit constants. The proofs of the published theorems that the archive formalises were not read; Section 10 identifies them. Qui Vincit directed the work and set its standards. Fable, a language model (Claude, Anthropic), ran the build, read the source and wrote the text, with further instances of the model reading bounded parts of the source; their reports are published with the paper. Reproducing the verification Everything needed to repeat Section 2 is in the repository https://github.com/vincitamore/riemann-lean-account together with the source of this paper, the reading notes, the delegated reports, the reviews of the replay program and the audit. The archive itself is not redistributed there; it is fetched from the author’s site and checked against the hash of Section 2. Requirements. The second build ran on a rented Linux machine with 32 threads, in a container whose memory was limited to 64 GB; the first on one with 32 threads limited to 128 GB. The build completed within each. The archive’s compiled files come to 100 GB. On the second machine the cone step took about 3.4 hours, by the times at which the files before and after it were written, and the replay 116 minutes on 32 threads; on the first, the replay with the earliest form of the program took 57 minutes on 28 threads, and the time of its cone step was not recorded. The scripts’ comments estimate 150 GB of free disk. The scripts in verify/ assume bash with git, curl, zstd, tar and sha256sum, and python3 for the scan; setup.sh installs elan if it is absent. Steps. (1) setup.sh downloads the fourteen parts, concatenates them, checks the SHA-256, unpacks, installs the pinned toolchain and compiles Mathlib from source at the pinned commit (or, on request, fetches its build cache). (2) scan.py is the static inspection of Section 2: it reads the downloaded file without unpacking it and needs nothing else. (3) toolchain.sh compares the installed toolchain with the release archive on GitHub. (4) build.sh runs lake build Main with every log line time-stamped and records the exit code. (5) janitor.sh must run beside the build. Lake writes, for every module, a file listing the module’s whole import closure, 5.9 MB on average here; for this tree those files would total about 1031 GB. The script deletes each one once its module’s compiled file exists,
THE ARGUMENT OF A FORMAL PROOF OF THE RIEMANN HYPOTHESIS 37 which Lake tolerates. With it the disk in use peaked at 115 GiB, as df read it once a minute. (6) summary.sh extracts from the logs and the built tree the facts of the build that this paper quotes. (7) check.sh prints the modules in which Mathlib declares its statement of the hypothesis, the zeta function and the L-function; the statements of the five theorems, with the archive’s definitions of them in full; and the axioms of the five theorems. It writes out Statements 2.1 to 2.3 and elaborates each afresh against its theorem, and it runs both checkers on the module Statements. (8) corollaries.sh compiles the Lean file of corollaries and the file of targets of Section 13 against the built archive and prints the axioms of the corollaries. (9) cut.sh builds and runs the program cut/CutMain.lean, which computes the depen- dency cone of Section 2.6 and writes the list of every declaration of the archive inside it and outside it. (10) replay.sh builds and runs the program replay/ReplayMain.lean, the kernel replay of Section 2.7. The number of threads is set with LEAN_NUM_THREADS. (11) hostile.sh builds the modules of hostile/ and runs Lean’s checker and the replay program on each (Table 1). (12) stock.sh runs leanchecker --fresh and then the replay program on Mathlib, or on the roots named in FRESH_ROOTS: with Main there, and another directory for the output, it is the check of the whole archive by Lean’s own checker. sample.sh runs leanchecker on a sample of names of the archive’s modules (Section 2.8). (13) independent.sh fetches lean4lean at a fixed commit, builds it with the archive’s toolchain, and runs it on the modules of hostile/, on the closures of the two modules of Mathlib named in Section 2.8 and on the same sample. (14) sample-exact.sh lists the modules under each name of the sample and, for a name on which a checker’s process was killed, calls that checker’s function for one module on each module the name covers, one at a time, after testing that way of calling it on two of the modules of hostile/. (15) mathlib-diff.sh fetches the community’s cached build of Mathlib into a second copy of the package and compares it with the build from source: the names of the constants, and the hashes of their statements and values (Section 2.4). (16) cone-probe.sh asks, for each constant which that comparison names, whether it is in the cone of riemannHypothesis. Its program, cut/ConeProbe.lean, computes the cone as the cone program does and prints its size. The outputs of our runs are in verify/results/, with an index that says what wrote each file; those of the first run are in verify/results/run1/. Every number in this paper that was measured is read from those files by paper/tools/numbers.py, which also checks that they agree with one another, apart from a few that no file holds and that are typed with their provenance in version.tex or, for the second machine’s price and start, in that program; and paper/tools/check_names.py checks every Lean name cited in the paper against the lists of declarations inside and outside the cone. Cost. The second run took 19 minutes to compile Mathlib and 16.0 hours to build the archive on 32 threads; the cone then took 3.4 hours and the replay 116 minutes. Lean’s own checker took 8.0 hours on one thread for the whole archive, and the replay, run again after it, 59 minutes. The provider billed 26.81 US dollars for the first machine. The second, at its hourly price, cost about 37 dollars from its start to the end of the steps reported here.
